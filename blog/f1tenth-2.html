F1Tenth Racecar (Senior Capstone) - Part II: Disparity+
I could still drive this with my eyes closed!
Cameron Lee, 6/5/2025 - 5:47pm
Preface

This year at SDSU, Dr. Bryan Donyanavard, offered a Senior Capstone class for computer science students. This was the first time this class had been offered and I got to be one of the test monkeys piloting the program. The class primarily focused on group collaboration, solving real problems, and getting a feel for what it's like to fulfill a product and present to a non-technical audience.

This is part 2 where I discuss the algorithm we started with and how we improved upon it.
Picking a Strategy
There are primarily two different methods one can take when approaching the problem of autonomous driving.

The first option is path planning. In order to plan a path, you first have to manually drive the robot around the track. LiDAR scans of the environment are then stitched together to create a virtual map.
Map being generated in real time via RViz

Once we have a map, we can calculate the "global trajectory" which is essentially the most optimal path to take around the track. With this, we can drop the robot into the environment we have mapped and use sensor information and localization algorithms to estimate where we are in the map. From there we can steer to fit the global trajectory. Lucky for us, Choi's existing team already had a working stack using this method, and before long, we were up and running.
Shoutout to my GOAT Professor Choi

Once we started running the car with the existing stack, we noticed two glaring issues:
First, and the most obvious, was how much time had to be put into setting up the system for a track. Every time we put the track back together, or moved to a new location, we had to manually drive the car to create a map and generate a new global trajectory before being able to test.
Alexa, play King of The Hill theme song
Secondly, once we started to crank up the speed, the localization got increasingly less accurate. Our baseline experiments showed that the speed capped out at about 2 meters per second before localization completely failed.
Clean maneuvers but not fast enough for a race


So, we looked toward a more reaction based approach. Rather than relying on a predefined trajectory and constantly trying to maintain that point, we can instead take in the sensor information and make decisions on how to steer in real time. In theory, this would be slower than taking the most optimal path; but, in practice, we can hit higher max speeds by not having to rely on complex computations.
Disparity Extender
Thanks to Jia's searching, we landed on the "Disparity Extender" algorithm. Disparity was used by a team from UNC-Chapel Hill that won an F1Tenth competition, held at CPSWeek 2019, in Montreal. I will briefly discuss how the algorithm works, but if you'd like a more in depth (and possibly more accurate) explanation, I highly encourage you to go check out their original blogpost.

The Disparity Extender algorithm is beautifully simple. There are only two main principles. 1: Find the longest "safe" path; and 2: Drive as fast as you can.

We determine what paths are considered "safe" by taking all of the LiDAR scans, using the disparities between the points in order to determine where obstacles start and end, then calculate how many lidar scans we need to exclude from that point in order to pad for the width of the car i.e. extending the disparities.
Graphic courtesy of Jia


Now we are left with an array of safe paths to take (assuming you've properly accounted for the width of the car) and we can shoot the car down the longest path as fast as we can. Theoretically, As long as we are doing this calculation frequently enough, we should change directions before ever approaching an obstacle, as long as a viable path can be seen; and, evidently this worked out great for the UNC-Chapel team. We however, would not be so fortunate as two major problems quickly presented themselves.
We don't have a ton of space in the RTEC Lab, especially since we share the space with another lab group at SDSU. It's plenty roomy for the lot of us working on software and simulators, but when it comes time to drive the car around it gets quite tight, especially when compared to the size of the competition tracks.
Losing my marbles after many late nights


Because of this, when we test, the car is constantly making really sharp and tight turns. With the car blindly driving at max speed, it could not turn nearly fast enough by the time a more optimal path presented itself. We were able to temporarily test the algorithm in our space by setting a cap to the maximum speed, but this made the car far too slow in long straightaways where there was more than enough room speed up.
When trying to tackle the opposite problem, large open spaces, we found that the algorithm could not properly decide which path to take. In this case, all of the LiDAR scans were an equal distance, causing somewhat undefined behavior. The car would unnecessarily change paths, swerve side to side, and in some cases completely spin out of control.
Thank goodness for simulators

Proportional Derivative Speed Controller
In order to fix the cornering issue, we needed a way to reign in the speed. Initially, we tried to just scale the speed proportionally to how long the path was, but this wasn't sufficient. If we tuned the value too high, then the car would slow down too soon. If we tuned the value too low, the car would not brake fast enough and smash through walls. So, the next simplest thing I could think of was adding the derivative component to the equation (some goold ol' robotics 101). By taking the derivative of the distance from a wall, we can get a value for how quickly the car is driving into it, allowing us to adaptively modulate the speed much more smoothly. If the car is approaching a corner slowly, speed can be maintained more. If the car is approaching a corner at high speeds, we need to brake harder. Before we knew it, our speed was governed by a PD controller!

Now that our speed was being updated potentially every cycle, the car suddenly became very jerky. With the beam constantly picking new paths and sensor noise, the car seemed to stutter a lot. In order to solve this, I implemented what has become one of my favorite solutions to throw at any real-time problem: the exponential moving average.

The idea behind the EMA is pretty simple. Instead of reacting immediately to every new measurement, we take a weighted average of our previous output and the new value. This means that sudden spikes in our sensor readings don't immediately become drastic changes in our speed. Instead, changes are gradually incorporated over subsequent cycles, smoothing out the response. Like whisking flour into a stew rather than dumping it all in at once. One or two outlier measurements can be largely inconsequential, while real obstacles that persist across several cycles have time to influence the output.
speed_filter.py 

SPEED_FILTER_OLD = 0.75
SPEED_FILTER_NEW = 0.25

filtered_speed = (
    (filtered_speed * SPEED_FILTER_OLD) +
    (new_speed * SPEED_FILTER_NEW)
)
The great part about this is that it allows us some flexibility with the weights between old and new sensor information. By weighing the previous value higher we trade responsiveness for stability and vice versa. We ended up using this both on the derivative of our PD controller and on the resulting speed command. Now, the car was running smooth like butter at high speeds.

Gaussian Steering Bias
So, like I mentioned previously, when the car is placed on a big track with several equally valued LiDAR scans, the logical decision would be to continue driving straight until a real informed decision can be made. However, because all of these paths are equally valued, the car has no real preference for one direction over another. As a result, even the slightest noise in sensor readings would cause it to suddenly favor a completely random path — swerving left and right for seemingly no reason, despite having plenty of room to drive straight

To fix this, we decided to introduce a little bias into our steering calculations. Using a Gaussian curve centered around the forward facing direction, we assign higher weights to paths closer to the center of our FOV and progressively lower weights to paths further away. If you've ever seen a bell curve, that's essentially what we're using here. A smooth, symmetrical curve that peaks in the middle and tapers off toward the sides. This way, when multiple paths are equally viable, the car naturally favors the one that keeps it driving straight but at the same time making sure we don't prevent the car from turning when necessary.
